Preface

At step 47,213 of a multi-week pretraining run, the loss spiked by a factor of 6. The curve had been smooth for weeks; the log showed a single cliff, then NaN, then an automatic job restart, and the checkpoint at step 47,000 turned out to be corrupted as well. Three engineers spent two days on the post-mortem. They checked gradient norms, batch statistics, data-pipeline checksums, the learning-rate warmup, and the optimizer state, and found nothing wrong with any of them. The cause, when they finally found it, was a four-byte integer.

Before the run started, a configuration change had halved the number of attention heads from 64 to 32 at fixed model width, which doubled the head dimension from 64 to 128. The 1/√dk scaling in the attention kernel read dk from a different config key, and that key still said 64. (This incident is a composite of several real ones, as are the other production stories in this book.)

The derivation that would have caught this is three equations long. It is in Section 11.2 of this book.

Derivation · Variance Analysis of the Scaling

Assume the entries qj and kj are independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Then

Var[q⊤k] = ∑j=1dk Var[qjkj] = dk(11.3)

Dividing by √dk restores unit variance, Var[q⊤k / √dk] = 1, and keeps the softmax out of saturation. Scale by √64 when dk = 128 and every logit is √2 too large.

Common Mistake · Two config keys, one quantity

Symptom. Weeks of smooth loss, then a single cliff and NaN.

Wrong hypothesis. A bad batch, or the optimizer state.

Root cause. The attention scale read a stale head dimension.

About the Author

Mikael Yemane built the first deep-learning predictive-maintenance system for the U.S. Navy’s F/A-18 Super Hornet fleet, and the Navy came to use it to determine the fleet’s mission capability. His computer-vision models at PG&E scanned drone imagery of California’s electrical grid for wildfire-ignition risk, and their output decided where inspection crews went first. Over seven years in production ML he has also built lead-ranking systems used by thousands of sellers at Microsoft and led MLOps for a federal agency’s models at Accenture. He now leads an ML team in aerospace as a Principal Machine Learning Engineer.

He has interviewed and onboarded many ML engineers. The ones who struggled knew the tools but not the math under them. This book derives that math, from linear algebra through Transformers, diffusion and RLHF, in roughly the order it comes up in engineering work. Every major algorithm has tested NumPy code in a free companion repository.
